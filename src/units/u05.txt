=== LESSON
## The big picture
The **French Revolution** (1789–1799) and the age of **Napoleon** (1799–1815) are the most heavily tested topics on this exam, about one question in nine. In 26 years France went from absolute monarchy to constitutional monarchy, to radical republic, to Terror, to a corrupt conservative republic, to military dictatorship, to empire, and back to a Bourbon king. Along the way it spread the ideas of **liberty, equality and nationalism** across Europe. Learn the sequence of phases; many questions ask which came first or what caused what.

## The Old Regime and its crisis
Before 1789, French society was divided into three legal orders called **estates**. The **First Estate** (clergy, under 1 percent of the population) and the **Second Estate** (nobility, about 2 percent) held privileges, above all freedom from most direct taxes such as the *taille*. The **Third Estate** was everyone else, about 97 percent: peasants, urban workers (the **sans-culottes**), and the wealthy, educated middle class, the **bourgeoisie** (merchants, lawyers, officials). The bourgeoisie resented being shut out of honors and offices by birth; peasants resented paying taxes, tithes and **seigneurial dues** to lords.

-> War debts from the Seven Years' War and the American Revolution; tax-exempt privileged orders | Royal bankruptcy forces Louis XVI to call the Estates-General (1789) | Third Estate seizes the initiative and turns a fiscal crisis into a revolution

The trigger was **money**. Wars, especially French aid to the American Revolution, had left the crown so indebted that interest payments ate about half the budget. Louis XVI's ministers proposed taxing the privileged; an **Assembly of Notables** (1787) and the Paris **parlement** refused and insisted that only the **Estates-General**, unused since 1614, could approve new taxes. A bad harvest in 1788 doubled bread prices just as elections were held. The abbé **Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès** captured the mood in his pamphlet ***What Is the Third Estate?*** (January 1789): "Everything. What has it been until now? Nothing."

## 1789: the year of revolution
The Estates-General met at Versailles in **May 1789**. The key fight was over voting. If each estate voted as a body, the two privileged orders could outvote the Third Estate 2 to 1; the Third wanted voting **by head**, since it had as many deputies as the other two combined. On **June 17** the Third Estate declared itself the **National Assembly**. Locked out of its hall, it met on a tennis court and swore the **Tennis Court Oath (June 20)** not to disband until France had a constitution.

When the king massed troops near Paris, crowds seeking gunpowder stormed the **Bastille**, a royal fortress-prison, on **July 14, 1789**. In the countryside, rumors of noble plots set off the **Great Fear**: peasants burned manor records of their dues. On the night of **August 4**, nobles in the Assembly gave up their feudal privileges. On **August 26** the Assembly adopted the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen**: men are born free and equal in rights; sovereignty rests in the nation; liberty, property, security and resistance to oppression are natural rights; all citizens are equal before the law and eligible for office by talent. In the **October Days**, thousands of Parisian women marched to Versailles over bread prices and forced the royal family to move to Paris.

## The moderate (constitutional) phase, 1789–1792
The National Assembly remade France on liberal lines. It replaced the old provinces with 83 **departments**, abolished guilds and internal tolls, and seized **Church lands** to pay the debt, issuing paper money (*assignats*) backed by them. The **Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790)** made priests state employees elected by citizens and required them to swear an oath to the constitution. The pope condemned it, about half the clergy refused the oath, and Catholic France split. This was the Revolution's first great mistake: it turned many devout peasants against it.

The **Constitution of 1791** created a **constitutional monarchy** with a one-house Legislative Assembly. Only "active citizens" who paid a certain level of tax could vote, so it was a government of property owners. **Olympe de Gouges** answered with a *Declaration of the Rights of Woman* (1791), demanding equal rights for women; she was executed in 1793.

-> The king's attempted flight (Varennes, June 1791) destroys trust; Austria and Prussia threaten intervention | France declares war on Austria (April 1792); Paris crowds overthrow the monarchy (August 1792) | A republic is proclaimed; radicals take power in wartime

In June 1791 Louis XVI tried to flee to the Austrian border and was caught at **Varennes**. Austria and Prussia's **Declaration of Pillnitz** (August 1791) hinted they would restore him. In **April 1792** the Assembly, eager to spread the Revolution, declared war on Austria. Early defeats and the Prussian commander's threat (the Brunswick Manifesto) to destroy Paris if the king were harmed led crowds to storm the Tuileries palace on **August 10, 1792**. In the **September Massacres**, mobs killed about 1,200 prisoners. A French victory at **Valmy** (September 20) stopped the invasion.

## The radical phase and the Terror, 1792–1794
A new assembly, the **National Convention**, elected by universal male suffrage, abolished the monarchy and declared France a **republic** (September 1792). It tried the king as "Citizen Louis Capet," and **Louis XVI was executed in January 1793**; Marie Antoinette followed in October. The Convention split between the more moderate **Girondins** and the radical **Jacobins** (the "Mountain"), who relied on the Paris sans-culottes. In June 1793 the Jacobins purged the Girondins.

France was at war with almost all of Europe, and the Catholic, royalist **Vendée** region rose in civil war. To survive, the Convention gave dictatorial power to the twelve-man **Committee of Public Safety**, dominated by **Maximilien Robespierre**. It ordered the **levée en masse** (August 1793), the first national draft: all citizens were mobilized for total war. A huge, motivated citizen army began to win. The Committee imposed price controls (the **Maximum**) on bread and other goods.

-> Foreign invasion, civil war in the Vendée, and fear of traitors | The Reign of Terror (September 1793–July 1794): Committee of Public Safety, Law of Suspects, guillotine | About 17,000 official executions; the Republic survives, but the Terror devours its leaders

The **Reign of Terror** used revolutionary tribunals and the **guillotine** against "enemies of the Republic": nobles, priests, Girondins, rebels, and eventually fellow Jacobins such as **Georges Danton**. About 17,000 people were officially executed and many more died in prison or in the brutal repression of the Vendée. Robespierre called terror "nothing but justice, prompt, severe, inflexible," an emanation of virtue. The radicals also tried to remake culture: **dechristianization** closed churches, a new **revolutionary calendar** dated years from the Republic's founding (Year I = 1792), and Robespierre staged a deist **Cult of the Supreme Being**.

## Europe reacts to the Revolution
The Revolution divided opinion everywhere. In Britain, the Irish-born politician **Edmund Burke** published ***Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790)**, predicting that tearing down ancient institutions in the name of abstract rights would end in bloodshed and dictatorship. Burke became the founder of modern **conservatism** (Unit 7). **Thomas Paine** answered with ***The Rights of Man* (1791)**, defending the Revolution and popular sovereignty. Governments in Britain, Austria, Prussia and Russia cracked down on radicals at home, and from 1792 they fought France in a series of **coalitions**. As French armies advanced, they set up "sister republics" (such as the Batavian Republic in the Netherlands) that abolished feudal privileges, which made the Revolution an international force.

## Thermidor and the Directory, 1794–1799
With the armies winning, the Terror seemed less necessary, and Convention members feared they were next. On 9 Thermidor (**July 27, 1794**) they arrested Robespierre and guillotined him the next day. This **Thermidorian Reaction** ended the Terror, closed the Jacobin Club and lifted price controls. The **Constitution of 1795** created the **Directory**: five executive Directors and a two-house legislature elected by property owners. The Directory was corrupt and unstable, beset by inflation, royalist and radical plots, and continuing war. It increasingly relied on the army, and especially on a young general from Corsica.

## Napoleon Bonaparte
**Napoleon Bonaparte** won fame by conquering northern Italy (1796–1797) and leading a campaign to **Egypt** (1798–1799), where scholars found the Rosetta Stone. In the **coup of 18 Brumaire (November 9, 1799)** he overthrew the Directory and became **First Consul**, then consul for life (1802) and finally **Emperor of the French (1804)**, crowning himself in Notre-Dame. Each step was approved by a **plebiscite**, a popular vote that gave dictatorship a democratic look.

At home Napoleon consolidated the moderate gains of the Revolution and ended its chaos:
- The **Concordat of 1801** with Pope Pius VII restored the Catholic Church in France while the state kept the confiscated Church lands and the right to nominate bishops. It made peace with Catholics.
- The **Napoleonic Code** (Civil Code, 1804) guaranteed **equality before the law**, the abolition of feudal privilege, secure **property rights** and religious freedom. It also restored patriarchal authority: wives had to obey husbands and lost rights to property, and it reinstated limits on workers. The Code spread across Europe and still underlies law in many countries.
- He created the **Bank of France**, a centralized administration of prefects, and elite secondary schools (*lycées*), and opened careers to talent. He also censored the press and used secret police.

## The Napoleonic Empire
-> Napoleon's victories over Austria, Prussia and Russia (1805–1807) | French domination of the continent; Continental System against Britain | Nationalist resistance (Spain, Germany) and the disastrous invasion of Russia lead to his fall

**Britain** remained his steadiest enemy. Admiral **Horatio Nelson** destroyed the French and Spanish fleets at **Trafalgar (1805)**, ending any hope of invading Britain. On land Napoleon seemed unbeatable: he crushed Austria and Russia at **Austerlitz (1805)**, abolished the **Holy Roman Empire (1806)** and grouped western German states into his **Confederation of the Rhine**, smashed Prussia at **Jena (1806)**, and made peace with Tsar Alexander I at **Tilsit (1807)**. He placed relatives on thrones in Spain, Naples, Holland and Westphalia and created the **Duchy of Warsaw** from Polish lands.

To defeat Britain economically, his **Continental System** (Berlin Decree, 1806) banned trade with Britain throughout Europe. It hurt European economies more than Britain's and caused resentment. When Portugal and later Russia broke it, Napoleon attacked them.

Two disasters followed. In Spain, after he made his brother Joseph king (1808), the Spanish people waged a savage **guerrilla** war (the **Peninsular War**, 1808–1814), aided by a British army under Wellington; the painter **Goya** recorded its horrors. In **1812** Napoleon invaded **Russia** with about 600,000 men. The Russians retreated, burned crops, and let him take a burned-out **Moscow**; when Alexander refused to negotiate, Napoleon retreated through the winter, and fewer than 100,000 of his soldiers got out.

A coalition of Russia, Prussia, Austria and Britain defeated him at **Leipzig, the "Battle of the Nations" (1813)**. Paris fell, and Napoleon abdicated in **1814** and was exiled to the island of **Elba**. The Bourbons returned under **Louis XVIII**. Napoleon escaped in 1815 for the **Hundred Days**, but was defeated by the Duke of **Wellington** and the Prussian Blücher at **Waterloo (June 18, 1815)** and exiled to remote **St. Helena**, where he died in 1821.

Napoleon's conquests spread revolutionary reforms (the Code, the end of feudal dues and guilds, religious toleration) across Europe. They also provoked **nationalism**: Spaniards, Germans and Russians learned to resist France as nations.

Revolutionary ideas also crossed the Atlantic. In the French colony of **Saint-Domingue**, enslaved people rose in 1791. Led by **Toussaint Louverture**, they forced France to abolish slavery (1794) and, after defeating an army Napoleon sent to restore it, declared the independent nation of **Haiti** in 1804, the first state founded by formerly enslaved people.

## The Congress of Vienna, 1814–1815
The victors met in Vienna to rebuild Europe. The leading figure was Austria's foreign minister, Prince **Klemens von Metternich**; others were Britain's **Castlereagh**, Russia's **Tsar Alexander I**, Prussia's Hardenberg, and the skillful French diplomat **Talleyrand**. Their goals were stability and preventing another French conquest.

-> Twenty-five years of revolution and war | Congress of Vienna: legitimacy, balance of power, containment of France | No general European war for a century (until 1914), but nationalism and liberalism are suppressed

They followed three principles:
- **Legitimacy:** restore "legitimate" dynasties, such as the Bourbons in France, Spain and Naples.
- **Balance of power:** no state should dominate. France was treated leniently so it would not seek revenge (in 1814 it kept its 1792 borders; after the Hundred Days it was cut back to roughly its 1790 borders, paid an indemnity and accepted a temporary army of occupation), but it was **ringed by buffer states**: the Kingdom of the **Netherlands** (with Belgium), a stronger **Piedmont-Sardinia**, and **Prussia's** new Rhineland provinces.
- **Compensation:** Austria got Lombardy-Venetia in Italy; Russia got most of Poland; Prussia got part of Saxony and the Rhineland; Britain kept colonies such as the Cape Colony and Ceylon.

Napoleon's German reorganization was kept in a new form: the **German Confederation** of 39 states under Austrian leadership. The great powers agreed to meet periodically to keep the peace, the **Concert of Europe** (Quadruple Alliance), and Alexander I proposed a **Holy Alliance** of Christian monarchs (Russia, Austria, Prussia). The settlement largely ignored nationalism and liberalism, which became the great forces of the next era (Unit 7).
