=== LESSON
## The big picture
After the Treaty of Utrecht (1713), Europe's great powers stopped fighting over religion and dynasties alone and started fighting over **trade and colonies**. Britain and France became global rivals, competing in the Caribbean, North America, India and on the seas. At home, Europe's population began a long climb that never stopped. This unit is small on the exam (about 5%), so focus on three things: the **Atlantic economy**, the **Anglo-French wars for empire**, and the **causes of population growth**.

## The Atlantic economy and the slave trade
In the 1700s the most valuable colonies were not in North America but in the **Caribbean**, where **sugar** plantations on islands such as French **Saint-Domingue** (today's Haiti) and British **Jamaica** made enormous profits. Sugar, tobacco, coffee, cotton and indigo were grown by **enslaved Africans**. Roughly six million Africans were carried across the Atlantic in the eighteenth century alone, more than in any other century of the trade, and many died on the brutal **Middle Passage**.

Historians describe a **triangular trade**: European manufactured goods (guns, cloth, alcohol) went to West Africa; enslaved people went to the Americas; plantation crops went back to Europe. Port cities such as Liverpool, Bristol, Nantes and Bordeaux grew rich on it. At Utrecht (1713) Britain had won the **asiento**, the contract to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America, later held by the South Sea Company.

-> Cheap plantation sugar, tea, coffee and tobacco flood into European ports | A "consumer revolution": ordinary Europeans buy colonial goods | Demand for slave labor and colonies grows; merchants and port cities gain wealth and influence

Governments still thought in **mercantilist** terms: colonies existed to enrich the mother country. Britain's **Navigation Acts** required colonial trade to travel in British ships, and colonists were expected to buy British manufactures. Spain, whose silver empire was in decline, attempted the **Bourbon reforms** to tighten control and revenue, and Portugal profited from gold discovered in **Brazil**.

## Europe after Utrecht, 1713–1740: a generation of (mostly) peace
**Britain.** In 1714 the German prince of Hanover became **George I**, under the Act of Settlement, which required a Protestant monarch. George spoke little English and left government to his ministers. **Robert Walpole** (in power 1721–1742) became Britain's first **prime minister** in all but name. He kept Britain out of wars, kept taxes low, and governed through a **cabinet** that needed the support of the House of Commons. That habit of ministers answering to Parliament became central to the British system.

**Financial bubbles.** In 1720 two speculative manias burst. In Britain the **South Sea Bubble** (shares in a company with a monopoly on trade with Spanish America) collapsed; Walpole rose by cleaning up the mess. In France, the Scottish financier **John Law** had set up a royal bank that issued paper money and a Mississippi Company promising profits from Louisiana. When his **Mississippi Bubble** burst in 1720, it ruined investors and left the French distrustful of paper money and banks for decades.

**France.** Louis XV was a child in 1715, so the Duke of Orléans ruled as regent, then **Cardinal Fleury** (1726–1743) gave France stable, peaceful government. The regent also returned to the Paris **parlement** (a high law court, not a legislature) its right to object to royal decrees. The parlements would use that power to block tax reform for the rest of the century.

**Austria.** Emperor **Charles VI** had no son. He spent his reign getting the powers of Europe to accept the **Pragmatic Sanction** (1713), a decree that his daughter **Maria Theresa** would inherit all the Habsburg lands undivided.

**Prussia.** **Frederick William I**, the "Soldier King" (r. 1713–1740), lived frugally, filled the treasury and built Europe's fourth-largest army from a small population, including a famous regiment of extremely tall soldiers. He rarely used the army. His son would.

## The wars for empire, 1740–1763
-> Charles VI dies (1740); Frederick II of Prussia sees a woman inherit Austria | Frederick seizes rich Silesia; War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748) | Prussia keeps Silesia; Austria wants revenge and Britain and France keep fighting overseas

In 1740 **Frederick II (the Great)** of Prussia ignored the Pragmatic Sanction and invaded **Silesia**, a rich Austrian province. France joined against Austria; Britain backed Austria. The **War of the Austrian Succession** (1740–1748) ended with the Treaty of **Aix-la-Chapelle**: Maria Theresa kept her throne, but Prussia kept Silesia.

Maria Theresa wanted Silesia back. In the **Diplomatic Revolution of 1756**, old enemies swapped partners: Austria allied with its traditional foe **France**, and Britain allied with **Prussia**. Russia joined Austria and France.

The **Seven Years' War** (1756–1763) was the first truly global war. In Europe, Prussia survived against huge odds (helped when the new Russian tsar, Peter III, an admirer of Frederick, made peace in 1762). Overseas, Britain, led by **William Pitt the Elder**, poured money into its navy and colonial campaigns. In North America (the "French and Indian War") the British took Quebec in 1759. In India, **Robert Clive** of the British East India Company won the **Battle of Plassey** (1757), beginning British control of Bengal. The **Treaty of Paris (1763)** gave Britain **Canada** and most of France's North American lands east of the Mississippi and left Britain the dominant power in India. France kept its profitable sugar islands.

-> Britain wins a global empire but doubles its national debt | Britain taxes the American colonies to pay for defense | Colonial resistance leads to the American Revolution (1775–1783)

The war's debts also crushed France. Its financial crisis would lead, a generation later, to 1789.

## Demographic change: Europe's population takes off
Europe's population grew from roughly **120 million in 1700 to about 190 million by 1800**, and growth accelerated afterward. The main causes were a **falling death rate**, not a rising birth rate:
- **Plague disappeared** from western Europe (the last major outbreak was at Marseille in 1720).
- **Fewer famines**, thanks to better farming, the spread of new American crops such as the **potato** and maize, and better roads and canals to move grain to hungry regions.
- Somewhat **better weather** after the cold of the seventeenth century and more effective governments controlling borders and epidemics.

Medicine played only a small role before the 1800s (Jenner's smallpox vaccine came only in 1796). The extra people needed work. Many rural families earned money in the **putting-out system** (cottage industry): a merchant delivered raw wool or cotton to a peasant household, the family spun and wove it at home, and the merchant collected and sold the cloth. Historians call this **proto-industrialization**, a step toward the factory system of the Industrial Revolution.

=== MUSTKNOW
- **Caribbean sugar** colonies (Saint-Domingue, Jamaica) were the most valuable colonies; worked by enslaved Africans; the 1700s were the peak century of the Atlantic slave trade.
- **Triangular trade:** manufactures to Africa, enslaved people to the Americas, plantation goods to Europe.
- **Walpole** (1721–42): Britain's first prime minister in practice; cabinet responsible to the Commons; peace and low taxes.
- **1720 bubbles:** the South Sea Bubble (Britain) and John Law's Mississippi Bubble (France).
- **Pragmatic Sanction:** Charles VI's plan for Maria Theresa to inherit undivided Habsburg lands.
- **1740:** Frederick the Great seizes Silesia → War of the Austrian Succession.
- **Diplomatic Revolution (1756):** Austria + France vs. Britain + Prussia.
- **Seven Years' War (1756–63)** → Treaty of Paris: Britain gains Canada and supremacy in India (Clive, Plassey 1757).
- **Population growth** came from a falling death rate: the end of plague, fewer famines, the potato and better transport, not from medicine.
- **Putting-out system** (cottage industry, proto-industrialization) employed the growing rural population.
