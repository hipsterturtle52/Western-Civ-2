=== LESSON
## The big picture
Between about 1543 and 1687, a handful of thinkers overturned a picture of the universe that Europeans had trusted for nearly 2,000 years. This **Scientific Revolution** changed not just astronomy but how educated people decided what was true: by **observation, experiment and mathematics** rather than by quoting ancient authorities or scripture. That new habit of mind led directly to the Enlightenment (Unit 4), so the exam often asks you to link a scientist to a discovery and a philosopher to a method.

## The old view: Aristotle, Ptolemy and Galen
Medieval universities taught a **geocentric** (Earth-centered) universe described by the Greek philosopher **Aristotle** and the astronomer **Ptolemy**. The Earth sat motionless at the center; the Moon, Sun, planets and stars moved around it on perfect crystalline spheres. The heavens were perfect and unchanging; the Earth was the realm of change and decay. This view fitted everyday experience (the Sun seems to move) and fitted Christian theology, which placed humanity at the center of creation. In medicine, the ancient physician **Galen** was the authority.

## From Copernicus to Newton: the key figures
**Nicolaus Copernicus**, a Polish cleric, published *On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres* in **1543**, the year he died. He proposed a **heliocentric** (Sun-centered) model. He kept circular orbits, so his system was not more accurate than Ptolemy's, but it was simpler, and it put the Earth in motion.

**Tycho Brahe**, a Danish astronomer, made the most precise naked-eye observations of the planets ever recorded. He did not accept full heliocentrism, but his data were priceless. His assistant **Johannes Kepler** used them to prove that planets move in **elliptical orbits**, not perfect circles, and stated three mathematical laws of planetary motion (first two published 1609). Kepler made the Sun-centered system accurate.

**Galileo Galilei** turned a new instrument, the **telescope**, on the sky in 1609–1610. He saw mountains on the Moon (the heavens were not perfect), moons orbiting **Jupiter** (not everything circled the Earth), and the phases of Venus (which made sense only if Venus orbited the Sun). He also studied motion with experiments, rolling balls down ramps, and developed the idea of **inertia**. In 1632 he published the *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*, which mocked the geocentric view. In **1633 the Roman Inquisition** forced him to recant and kept him under house arrest for life. His case became the symbol of conflict between science and church authority.

-> Copernicus, Kepler and Galileo make the old Earth-centered physics impossible | Newton's Principia (1687) explains heaven and earth with one set of laws | The universe looks like a rational machine; reason seems able to explain everything

**Isaac Newton** pulled everything together. His *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*, the **Principia (1687)**, set out three laws of motion and the **law of universal gravitation**: the same force that makes an apple fall keeps the Moon in orbit and the planets around the Sun. Newton also co-invented **calculus** (independently of the German **Leibniz**). The Newtonian universe was a vast machine running on mathematical laws. Many people concluded that God was a great designer, a "clockmaker," who built the machine and let it run.

Science advanced in other fields too. **Andreas Vesalius** published *On the Fabric of the Human Body* (also **1543**), based on dissecting human corpses, correcting Galen. **William Harvey** showed in 1628 that the heart pumps blood in a **circulation** through the body. **Robert Boyle** studied gases (**Boyle's law**) and attacked the old idea of four elements. **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek** used microscopes to see bacteria and sperm cells.

## The new method: Bacon and Descartes
Two philosophers explained how to find truth.

**Francis Bacon** (English) championed **empiricism** and the **inductive method**: gather many observations and experiments, then build general conclusions from them. Knowledge, he said, should be useful and should give humans power over nature. His *Novum Organum* appeared in 1620.

**René Descartes** (French) championed **rationalism** and **deduction**: start from certain first principles and reason step by step. Doubting everything, he found one thing he could not doubt: "**I think, therefore I am**" (*Discourse on Method*, 1637). He split reality into mind and matter (**Cartesian dualism**) and invented analytic geometry, which joins algebra and geometry.

-> Old authorities (Aristotle, Galen) are shown to be wrong | Bacon's experiments and Descartes's reasoning offer new ways to know | The **scientific method** combines observation with mathematics; trust shifts from tradition to evidence

Modern science combines both: Bacon's experiments and Descartes's mathematical reasoning. Governments began to sponsor research through new academies: the **Royal Society of London** (chartered 1662) and the **French Academy of Sciences** (1666). A few women took part despite exclusion from universities, such as the astronomer **Maria Winkelmann**, refused a post at the Berlin Academy, and the writer **Margaret Cavendish**, who visited the Royal Society.

## New knowledge of man and society
Scientific thinking soon turned to human affairs. **John Locke's** *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690) argued that the mind at birth is a **tabula rasa**, a blank slate, filled only by experience. If people are shaped by environment, then better institutions and education can make better people. That idea powered Enlightenment reform. Skeptics such as **Pierre Bayle** (*Historical and Critical Dictionary*, 1697) argued that no belief should be accepted without evidence and pleaded for religious toleration. The Dutch philosopher **Baruch Spinoza** identified God with nature. Travel accounts of China, the Americas and Persia showed that other societies had their own morals and customs, encouraging cultural **relativism**. Educated elites also stopped believing in witchcraft, and the great witch hunts faded by about 1700.

## Political theory: Hobbes and Locke
Both men used the idea of a **state of nature** (life before government) and a **social contract** (the agreement that creates government), but they reached opposite conclusions.

**Thomas Hobbes**, shaken by the English Civil War, wrote ***Leviathan* (1651)**. In the state of nature, he said, humans are selfish and fearful, and life is "**solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short**." To escape, people give up their rights to an all-powerful sovereign. Rebellion is never justified, because any government is better than chaos. Hobbes defended absolute power, but on rational grounds, not divine right.

**John Locke** wrote ***Two Treatises of Government* (1690)** to justify the Glorious Revolution. In his state of nature people are reasonable and already possess **natural rights to life, liberty and property**. They create government only to protect those rights. A government that violates them breaks the contract, and the people have a **right of revolution**. Locke's ideas later shaped the American Declaration of Independence.

The Dutch jurist **Hugo Grotius** (*On the Law of War and Peace*, 1625) argued that **natural law** binds nations as well as individuals; he is called the father of international law.
