=== LESSON
## The big picture
The twenty years between the world wars began with hopes for democracy and ended in dictatorship and war. Democracies were born weak, especially Germany's **Weimar Republic**; the **Great Depression** after 1929 shattered faith in liberal capitalism; and new **totalitarian** regimes, **Mussolini's Fascist Italy, Hitler's Nazi Germany and Stalin's Soviet Union**, claimed total control over their citizens. Meanwhile Britain and France, exhausted and fearful of another war, tried **appeasement**, which failed. On the exam, know how each dictator rose to power, what each regime did, and the steps from Versailles to September 1939.

## Postwar crises and the Weimar Republic
Germany's new democracy, the **Weimar Republic** (named for the town where its constitution was written in 1919), was blamed from the start for signing the hated Treaty of Versailles. Nationalists spread the "**stab-in-the-back**" myth that the army had not been defeated but betrayed by socialists, Jews and democratic politicians at home. The republic faced attacks from the left (the communist **Spartacist uprising**, January 1919, in which Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht were killed) and the right (the **Kapp Putsch**, 1920). Its constitution used pure **proportional representation**, producing many small parties and unstable coalitions, and **Article 48** let the president rule by emergency decree.

-> Germany falls behind on reparations | France and Belgium occupy the Ruhr (1923); Germany prints money to pay striking workers | Hyperinflation wipes out middle-class savings; extremists gain, including Hitler's failed Beer Hall Putsch

In **1923**, when Germany fell behind on reparations, **France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr**, Germany's industrial heartland. The government supported a general strike of "passive resistance" by printing money, and **hyperinflation** exploded: by November a U.S. dollar cost **4.2 trillion marks**. Savings, pensions and the security of the middle class evaporated. In Munich, **Adolf Hitler** tried to seize power in the **Beer Hall Putsch** (November 1923); it failed, and in prison he wrote ***Mein Kampf*** (*My Struggle*).

Recovery came under Foreign Minister **Gustav Stresemann**. A new currency ended inflation; the **Dawes Plan (1924)** reduced reparations payments and brought **American loans**; at **Locarno (1925)** Germany accepted its western borders, and in 1926 it joined the **League of Nations**. In the **Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928)**, dozens of nations renounced war (with no means of enforcement). The years 1924–1929 were Weimar's "golden twenties," but prosperity rested on borrowed American money.

**Culture** in the 1920s reflected both disillusionment and experiment. T. S. Eliot's poem *The Waste Land* (1922) and novels of the war's "lost generation" expressed despair; Oswald Spengler predicted *The Decline of the West*. Artists in the **Dada** movement mocked reason and art itself; the **Surrealists** explored dreams and Freud's unconscious; James Joyce (*Ulysses*, 1922) and Virginia Woolf used "stream of consciousness"; Franz Kafka depicted people trapped by faceless bureaucracy. The **Bauhaus** school under Walter Gropius developed functional modern architecture and design. Radio, cinema and jazz created a new mass culture, and governments soon learned to use them for propaganda.

## Fascist Italy
Italy was on the winning side in 1918 but felt cheated of promised territory (a "**mutilated victory**"). Strikes, factory occupations and peasant land seizures in 1919–1920 (the "red years") frightened landowners and industrialists. **Benito Mussolini**, a former socialist journalist, founded the **Fascist** movement in 1919 (from *fasces*, the ancient Roman symbol of authority). His **Blackshirt** squads beat socialists and broke strikes, often with police tolerance.

-> Postwar discontent, strikes and fear of socialist revolution; weak coalition governments | March on Rome (1922): the king appoints Mussolini prime minister | By 1926 a one-party dictatorship; the first fascist state

In October 1922 Fascists threatened a **March on Rome**. Rather than use the army, **King Victor Emmanuel III** appointed Mussolini **prime minister**. Mussolini came to power legally, then dismantled democracy: an electoral law gave the largest party two-thirds of the seats; Fascists murdered the socialist deputy **Giacomo Matteotti** (1924), who had denounced their violence; and by 1926 opposition parties were banned and the press censored. As **Il Duce** ("the leader"), Mussolini declared: "**Everything within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state**." His **corporate state** grouped employers and workers into state-controlled corporations, banning independent unions and strikes. The **Lateran Accords (1929)** made peace with the Church, recognizing **Vatican City** as independent and Catholicism as the state religion. In 1935–1936 Italy conquered **Ethiopia**, defying the League of Nations.

**Fascism** in general meant: extreme **nationalism**, a **single party** and cult of the leader, glorification of **violence and war**, hatred of **communism** and contempt for **liberal democracy**, and a state that claimed to embody the nation, while leaving private property and big business in place.

## The Great Depression
-> U.S. stock market crash (October 1929); American loans to Europe are recalled | Banks fail (Austria's Creditanstalt, 1931); trade collapses behind tariffs; mass unemployment | Voters turn to extremes; democracies are discredited, especially in Germany

The **Wall Street Crash of October 1929** began a worldwide depression. American lenders recalled their loans to Europe; in 1931 Austria's largest bank, the **Creditanstalt**, collapsed, setting off banking panics across central Europe. Countries raised tariffs (the U.S. Smoot-Hawley Tariff, 1930), and world trade shrank by about two-thirds. Unemployment reached about **6 million in Germany** (roughly a third of workers) by 1932, and around 3 million in Britain. Britain left the **gold standard** in 1931.

Most governments first cut spending to balance budgets, which deepened the slump. The British economist **John Maynard Keynes** (*The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, 1936) argued instead that governments should **spend and run deficits** in a depression to create demand. In France, the left-wing **Popular Front** government of **Léon Blum** (1936) introduced the 40-hour week and paid vacations. In **Sweden**, Social Democrats built a welfare state with public works, a "middle way" between capitalism and socialism.

## Nazi Germany
**Adolf Hitler**, an Austrian-born veteran, led the **National Socialist German Workers' (Nazi) Party**. Nazi ideology combined extreme nationalism with **racism**: Germans were a superior "Aryan" race, **Jews** were blamed for every disaster from the defeat of 1918 to communism and capitalism, and Germany needed "**living space**" (*Lebensraum*) in eastern Europe. Nazis promised a unified "people's community" (*Volksgemeinschaft*) led by an all-powerful **Führer**.

-> The Depression brings mass unemployment; Weimar governments rule by decree | Nazis become the largest party (37 percent, July 1932); conservatives persuade Hindenburg to appoint Hitler chancellor (January 30, 1933) | Reichstag fire decree and Enabling Act (1933) create a legal dictatorship within months

In 1928 the Nazis won under 3 percent of the vote. The Depression changed everything: in **July 1932** they won **37 percent**, becoming the largest party in the Reichstag. Hitler never won a majority. Conservative politicians around President **Paul von Hindenburg**, led by Franz von Papen, believed they could control him, and on **January 30, 1933**, Hindenburg appointed Hitler **chancellor**. Hitler came to power legally.

He then destroyed democracy in months. After the **Reichstag fire** (February 1933), which Nazis blamed on communists, an emergency decree suspended civil liberties. The **Enabling Act (March 1933)** let Hitler's cabinet make laws without the Reichstag for four years. The Nazis banned other parties and trade unions and brought every organization under party control (*Gleichschaltung*, "coordination"). In the **Night of the Long Knives (June 1934)**, Hitler had the SS murder the leaders of his own storm troopers (the SA), including Ernst Röhm, to reassure the army. When Hindenburg died in August 1934, Hitler combined the offices of president and chancellor as **Führer**. The **SS** under Heinrich Himmler and the secret police (**Gestapo**) enforced terror; Joseph **Goebbels** ran propaganda; the **Hitler Youth** shaped the young; women were urged to focus on "children, kitchen, church."

Persecution of Jews escalated in stages: boycotts of Jewish shops (1933), dismissal from the civil service, the **Nuremberg Laws (1935)**, which stripped Jews of citizenship and banned marriage between Jews and other Germans, and the **Kristallnacht** pogrom (**November 1938**), when Nazis burned synagogues, smashed Jewish businesses and sent about 30,000 Jewish men to concentration camps. Public works, **rearmament** and conscription ended mass unemployment by 1936–37, which won Hitler wide support.

## Stalin's Soviet Union
After Lenin died in 1924, **Joseph Stalin**, the party's **general secretary**, used his control over appointments to defeat his rivals. **Leon Trotsky** argued for "**permanent revolution**" spreading communism worldwide; Stalin championed "**socialism in one country**," building up the USSR first. Trotsky was expelled in 1929 and murdered in Mexico in 1940 by a Soviet agent.

-> Stalin wants rapid industrialization to make the USSR a military-industrial power | Five-Year Plans (from 1928) and forced collectivization of agriculture (from 1929) | Huge industrial growth, but millions die in famine (1932–33), and terror becomes a system of rule

In 1928 Stalin ended the NEP and launched the first **Five-Year Plan**, setting impossible targets for **heavy industry** (steel, coal, electricity, tractors) under a **command economy** run by the state planning agency. New industrial cities like Magnitogorsk rose from nothing; workers like the miner Aleksei Stakhanov were held up as heroes. To feed the cities and pay for machines, Stalin forced peasants into **collective farms** (*kolkhozes*). Better-off peasants, the **kulaks**, were "liquidated as a class": shot, deported or sent to the **Gulag** labor camps. Peasants slaughtered livestock rather than give them up. The resulting **famine of 1932–1933** killed millions, most terribly in **Ukraine** (the Holodomor).

In the **Great Purge (Great Terror, 1936–1938)**, Stalin's secret police (the **NKVD**) arrested millions. Old Bolsheviks confessed to absurd crimes in **show trials**; most of the army's top officers were shot; roughly 700,000 people were executed in 1937–38 alone, and many more died in the Gulag. A **cult of personality** glorified Stalin, and the arts had to follow **socialist realism**, depicting heroic workers building socialism.

**Totalitarianism**, a term used for Fascist Italy, Nazi Germany and the Stalinist USSR, describes a regime that seeks **total control** of public and private life through a single party, an official ideology, mass propaganda, secret police terror and control of the economy and culture. The regimes' goals differed (communism aimed at a classless society; Nazism at racial empire), but their methods were strikingly similar.

## The road to war: international politics in the 1930s
The **League of Nations** had no army and depended on the great powers, which would not act. It failed when **Japan invaded Manchuria (1931)** and when **Italy invaded Ethiopia (1935)**; its sanctions on Italy excluded oil and were soon dropped.

-> Hitler tears up Versailles step by step while Britain and France, fearing another war, appease him | Rhineland (1936), Anschluss (1938), Munich and the Sudetenland (1938), Prague (March 1939) | Britain and France guarantee Poland; the Nazi-Soviet Pact frees Hitler to invade Poland (September 1, 1939)

Hitler's steps:
- **1933:** Germany leaves the League of Nations. **1935:** Hitler announces **rearmament** and conscription, openly breaking Versailles; the Saar votes to rejoin Germany.
- **March 1936:** German troops **remilitarize the Rhineland**. France and Britain do nothing.
- **1936:** Germany and Italy form the **Rome-Berlin Axis**; Germany and Japan sign the **Anti-Comintern Pact**. In the **Spanish Civil War (1936–1939)**, Germany and Italy aid General **Francisco Franco's** Nationalists, while the USSR aids the Republic; Britain and France stay neutral. German bombers destroy the Basque town of **Guernica** (1937), immortalized in Picasso's painting. Franco wins in 1939.
- **March 1938:** the **Anschluss**, annexation of **Austria**.
- **September 1938:** Hitler demands the **Sudetenland**, the German-speaking border region of Czechoslovakia. At the **Munich Conference**, British Prime Minister **Neville Chamberlain** and French premier Daladier agree to give it to Germany; Czechoslovakia is not consulted. Chamberlain claims "**peace for our time**."
- **March 1939:** Germany occupies the rest of **Czechoslovakia**, showing that Hitler's aims went beyond uniting Germans. Britain and France **guarantee Poland**.
- **August 23, 1939:** the **Nazi-Soviet Nonaggression Pact**. Its secret protocol divided Poland and eastern Europe between Hitler and Stalin.
- **September 1, 1939:** Germany invades **Poland**; Britain and France declare war on September 3.

Why **appeasement**? Memories of 1914–1918, a belief that Versailles had been unfair to Germany, fear of communism, military unpreparedness, the Depression's strain on budgets, and the hope that Hitler's demands were limited.
