=== LESSON
## The big picture
The **Enlightenment** was an eighteenth-century movement that applied the methods of the Scientific Revolution to human society. If Newton could find the natural laws of the universe, the thinkers of the Enlightenment asked, why not find the natural laws of government, economics, religion and justice? Their tools were **reason, skepticism of tradition, and confidence in progress**. Their targets were the Catholic Church's power, censorship, torture, arbitrary rule and inherited privilege. The exam loves to match each thinker to a book and an idea, so learn the "who said what" list in the Must-know box.

## The philosophes and the spread of ideas
The French called these writers **philosophes**: not academic philosophers but public intellectuals who wrote clearly for educated readers. Their ideas spread through new channels of the **public sphere**: books and newspapers, coffeehouses, reading clubs, Masonic lodges and the **salons**, gatherings hosted in Paris homes by educated women such as **Madame Geoffrin**, where nobles, writers and officials debated ideas. Censorship existed but was often evaded by printing in the Dutch Republic or Switzerland.

-> Newton shows nature runs on rational laws; Locke says people are shaped by environment | Philosophes apply reason to society and attack tradition, privilege and the Church | Reform programs: toleration, legal reform, limited or "enlightened" government, and eventually revolution

The great collective project was the ***Encyclopedia***, edited by **Denis Diderot** and Jean d'Alembert (published 1751–1772, 28 volumes). It gathered practical and scientific knowledge, from how to make pins to how to think about religion, and quietly criticized the Church and the state. The French government banned it more than once, but it was completed anyway.

## The great thinkers
**Voltaire** (François-Marie Arouet, 1694–1778) was the most famous philosophe. He admired England's tolerance and freedoms (*Letters on the English*, 1733), mocked religious fanaticism, and campaigned to clear the name of **Jean Calas**, a Protestant executed after a biased trial. His satire ***Candide*** (1759) ridiculed blind optimism. His battle cry against the intolerant Church was "**Écrasez l'infâme**" ("Crush the infamous thing"). He was a **deist**, like many philosophes: God created the universe and its laws, then let it run; there is no need for miracles, priests or revealed scripture.

**Baron de Montesquieu** wrote ***The Spirit of the Laws* (1748)**. Studying governments around the world, he argued that liberty is safest when power is divided among **legislative, executive and judicial branches** that check one another: the **separation of powers**. He thought England's system came closest. The framers of the U.S. Constitution relied heavily on him. His earlier *Persian Letters* satirized French society through the eyes of fictional Persian visitors.

**Jean-Jacques Rousseau** was the Enlightenment's most radical and most unusual voice. In ***The Social Contract* (1762)** he wrote, "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." Legitimate government, he argued, must express the **general will**, the collective will of the community for the common good, and individuals may be "forced to be free" by obeying it. That idea could support democracy (popular sovereignty) or justify majority tyranny. In *Emile* (1762) he argued that children should learn by nature and experience. Rousseau distrusted pure reason and praised emotion and nature, so he also anticipated **Romanticism** (Unit 7).

**Adam Smith**, a Scottish economist, wrote ***The Wealth of Nations* (1776)**, the founding work of modern economics. He attacked mercantilism and argued for **laissez-faire** ("let it be") free markets: when individuals pursue their own interest, an "**invisible hand**" of competition promotes the general good. He praised the **division of labor**, showing how a pin factory multiplied output by splitting work into simple steps. The French **physiocrats** (such as François Quesnay) had argued earlier that land, not gold, is the source of wealth and that trade in grain should be free.

**Cesare Beccaria**, an Italian, wrote ***On Crimes and Punishments* (1764)**, arguing against **torture** and the **death penalty** and for punishments that are swift, certain and proportional to the crime. Many states reformed their laws because of him.

**Mary Wollstonecraft** wrote ***A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792)**, arguing that women appear inferior only because they are denied education, and that reason belongs to both sexes. Most male philosophes, including Rousseau, assumed women belonged in the home.

**David Hume**, a Scottish philosopher, pushed skepticism further, questioning cause and effect and arguing that reports of miracles are never believable. **Immanuel Kant** of Prussia summed up the age in his 1784 essay "What Is Enlightenment?": "**Dare to know!**" (*Sapere aude*), that is, have the courage to use your own reason.

## Enlightened despotism
Some monarchs, called **enlightened despots** (or enlightened absolutists), borrowed Enlightenment ideas to make their states stronger and more efficient, without giving up any power.

-> Monarchs want stronger, richer states after costly wars | Enlightened despots adopt rational reforms from the philosophes | Real gains in law, toleration and administration, but no sharing of power and serfdom mostly untouched

**Frederick II (the Great) of Prussia** (r. 1740–1786) corresponded with Voltaire and hosted him at his palace. He called himself "the **first servant of the state**," abolished torture, granted wide religious toleration, reformed the courts and promoted agriculture. But he kept serfdom and relied on the Junkers to staff his army.

**Catherine II (the Great) of Russia** (r. 1762–1796) corresponded with Voltaire and Diderot and called a Legislative Commission in 1767 to reform Russian law. After the huge peasant revolt led by **Emelian Pugachev** (1773–1775), she sided firmly with the nobles: her **Charter of the Nobility (1785)** freed nobles from state service and confirmed their power over serfs. Serfdom expanded under her rule. She also expanded Russia south to the Black Sea at the expense of the Ottomans.

**Joseph II of Austria** (r. 1780–1790) was the most radical. His **Edict of Toleration (1781)** gave rights to Protestants and Orthodox Christians, and he eased restrictions on Jews. He **abolished serfdom** and tried to replace labor services with money rents, closed hundreds of monasteries and imposed German as the language of administration. Nobles, clergy and non-German peoples resisted so fiercely that his successor, Leopold II, cancelled most of his reforms. Joseph's failure shows how hard it was to impose reform from above.

## Toleration, reform and the limits of the Enlightenment
Toleration was the cause that united almost every philosophe. Religious wars and persecution, they argued, came from **fanaticism** and **superstition**, and a rational state should leave belief to the individual. The German Jewish philosopher **Moses Mendelssohn** argued that Jews could be loyal citizens and full participants in European culture, and his friend Gotthold Lessing's play *Nathan the Wise* (1779) pleaded for respect among Jews, Christians and Muslims. Some states moved slowly toward legal equality: Joseph II eased restrictions on Jews in the 1780s, and revolutionary France granted Jews full citizenship in 1791.

Enlightenment writers also began to attack **slavery**. Montesquieu ridiculed the arguments used to defend it, the *Encyclopedia* condemned it, and by the 1780s abolition societies had formed in Britain and France. Their success came later: Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807.

Yet the Enlightenment had clear limits. Most philosophes were **reformers, not democrats**. Voltaire hoped for a wise king, not rule by the uneducated masses. Many assumed that women's nature suited them for the home, and some, including Hume, wrote that non-Europeans were inferior. Critics then and since have pointed out that "reason" was often defined by and for educated European men.

-> Philosophes teach that institutions should be judged by reason and utility | Readers begin to question privilege, censorship and arbitrary power | In 1789 French revolutionaries put Enlightenment principles (rights, popular sovereignty, separation of powers) into practice

The Enlightenment's most lasting legacy is the idea of **progress**: that human beings can use reason to understand their world and make it better. Its political ideas (natural rights, separation of powers, popular sovereignty, toleration, free markets) became the program of the **American and French Revolutions** (Unit 5) and of nineteenth-century **liberalism** (Unit 7).

## The partitions of Poland
**Poland-Lithuania** was a large but weak state. Its nobles elected the king, and any single noble could block legislation in the parliament (the **liberum veto**). Its neighbors exploited the weakness.

-> Poland's elected kings and the liberum veto leave it with no strong government or army | Russia, Prussia and Austria agree to take Polish territory in 1772, 1793 and 1795 | Poland disappears from the map until 1918

In the **First Partition (1772)**, Russia, Prussia and Austria took about a third of its territory. Poland tried to reform and adopted a modern **constitution in May 1791**. Russia and Prussia answered with the **Second Partition (1793)**. A national uprising led by **Tadeusz Kościuszko** failed, and the **Third Partition (1795)** erased Poland entirely. The partitions show that "enlightened" rulers still practiced old-fashioned power politics.
