=== LESSON
## The big picture
After 1848 failed, nationalism did not die; it changed hands. Instead of idealistic students and poets, hard-headed statesmen used armies and diplomacy, a style called **Realpolitik** ("the politics of reality"), to build nation-states. In just twelve years **Italy (1861)** and **Germany (1871)** were unified, the map of Europe was redrawn, and a newly powerful Germany sat at the center of the continent. Meanwhile the old multinational empires (Austria-Hungary, Russia, the Ottoman Empire) struggled to reform, democracies expanded the vote in Britain and France, and the workers' movement grew into mass socialist parties. The period ends with Europe divided into two armed alliance blocs.

## The Crimean War (1853–1856): the Concert breaks
Russia pressed the weakening **Ottoman Empire** (the "sick man of Europe"), claiming to protect Orthodox Christians. Britain and France, fearing Russian control of the Straits and the eastern Mediterranean, went to war alongside the Ottomans; little **Piedmont-Sardinia** joined too. Most fighting centered on the siege of the Russian naval base at **Sevastopol** in the Crimea. The war was notoriously mismanaged (the Charge of the Light Brigade) and killed hundreds of thousands, many from disease; **Florence Nightingale's** nursing reforms made her famous.

-> Russian pressure on the Ottoman Empire alarms Britain and France | Crimean War (1853–56); Russia defeated; Treaty of Paris (1856) | Concert of Europe shattered; Austria isolated; Russia turns to internal reform; the way opens for Italian and German unification

The **Treaty of Paris (1856)** neutralized the Black Sea. More important were the side effects: the great powers no longer cooperated to keep the 1815 order, **Austria** had angered Russia by not helping it, and Russia began the Great Reforms. Without the Concert, Cavour and Bismarck could redraw the map.

## The unification of Italy
In 1850 Italy was a patchwork: Austria held **Lombardy and Venetia**, the pope ruled the **Papal States**, Bourbon kings ruled the **Kingdom of the Two Sicilies** (Naples and Sicily), and the only independent, constitutional Italian state was **Piedmont-Sardinia** under King **Victor Emmanuel II**.

Its prime minister, Count **Camillo di Cavour** (from 1852), was a liberal and a master of Realpolitik. He modernized Piedmont's economy and railways and sent troops to the Crimean War to win a seat at the peace conference. He knew Piedmont could not beat Austria alone, so at **Plombières (1858)** he secretly won French help from **Napoleon III** in exchange for Nice and Savoy.

-> Cavour provokes Austria into war (1859) with French support | Victories at Magenta and Solferino; Piedmont gains Lombardy; central Italy votes to join; Garibaldi conquers the south (1860) | Kingdom of Italy proclaimed (1861); Venetia added 1866, Rome 1870

In the **war of 1859**, French and Piedmontese armies won at **Magenta** and **Solferino**, and Piedmont gained **Lombardy**. (The bloodshed at Solferino inspired the founding of the **Red Cross**.) The duchies of central Italy overthrew their rulers and voted by **plebiscite** to join Piedmont; Cavour paid Napoleon III with **Nice and Savoy**.

Then **Giuseppe Garibaldi**, a romantic republican guerrilla, sailed with about 1,000 volunteers, the "**Red Shirts**," and conquered **Sicily and Naples** (1860). Cavour, fearing Garibaldi would march on Rome and provoke France, sent Piedmont's army through the Papal States to meet him. Garibaldi, a patriot first, **handed his conquests to Victor Emmanuel**. In **March 1861** the **Kingdom of Italy** was proclaimed. **Venetia** was added in **1866** (Italy allied with Prussia against Austria), and **Rome** in **1870**, when French troops guarding the pope left to fight Prussia. Rome became the capital; the pope refused to recognize the kingdom and called himself the "prisoner of the Vatican."

Unity did not end Italy's problems: a deep gap between the industrializing **north** and the poor, rural **south**, hostility from the Church, and a narrow electorate. As one politician put it, "We have made Italy; now we must make Italians."

## The unification of Germany
-> Prussian king and parliament deadlock over army reform (1862) | Bismarck wins three short wars: Denmark (1864), Austria (1866), France (1870–71) | German Empire proclaimed at Versailles (January 1871), dominated by Prussia; France loses Alsace-Lorraine and wants revenge

Prussia had advantages: a strong army, the **Zollverein** customs union that already tied most German states to its economy, and booming coal and steel industries. In 1862 King **William I** appointed **Otto von Bismarck** minister-president to push through an army expansion that the liberal parliament refused to fund. Bismarck simply collected the taxes anyway, declaring that the great questions of the day would be decided "not by speeches and majority decisions, but by **iron and blood**."

Bismarck unified Germany through **three wars**:
1. The **Danish War (1864)**: Prussia and Austria together took **Schleswig and Holstein** from Denmark. Their joint administration gave Bismarck a ready-made quarrel with Austria.
2. The **Austro-Prussian (Seven Weeks') War (1866)**: after making sure France and Russia would stay out and allying with Italy, Bismarck crushed Austria at **Königgrätz (Sadowa)**. He imposed a lenient peace to avoid lasting hatred, but **Austria was excluded from German affairs**. Prussia annexed several states and formed the **North German Confederation (1867)**. Prussian liberals, dazzled by victory, forgave Bismarck's illegal taxes.
3. The **Franco-Prussian War (1870–1871)**: a dispute over a Hohenzollern candidate for the Spanish throne gave Bismarck his chance. He edited the **Ems Dispatch**, a telegram about a meeting between William I and the French ambassador, to make it sound insulting; France declared war. The southern German states joined Prussia; the French army collapsed, and **Napoleon III was captured at Sedan** (September 1870). Paris was besieged and surrendered in January 1871.

On **January 18, 1871**, in the **Hall of Mirrors at Versailles**, William I was proclaimed **German Emperor (Kaiser)**. In the peace, France paid a large indemnity and lost **Alsace-Lorraine**, leaving a lasting French desire for revenge (*revanche*). Germany was unified "from above," by Prussian arms and princes rather than by a liberal parliament.

The new **German Empire** was federal and dominated by Prussia. The **Reichstag** was elected by **universal male suffrage**, but the **chancellor** answered to the Kaiser, not to the Reichstag. As chancellor (1871–1890), Bismarck fought the Catholic Church in the **Kulturkampf** ("culture struggle," 1870s), then turned against the socialists with the **Anti-Socialist Laws (1878)**. To win workers away from socialism he also created the world's first national **social insurance**: health insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884), and old-age and disability pensions (1889). In 1888 **William II** became Kaiser; he dismissed Bismarck in **1890** and pursued a restless **Weltpolitik** ("world policy"), including a big navy that alarmed Britain.

## Austria-Hungary
Defeats in 1859 and 1866 forced the Habsburgs to compromise with their largest and most assertive nationality, the **Magyars** (Hungarians). The **Compromise (Ausgleich) of 1867** created the **Dual Monarchy of Austria-Hungary**: Franz Joseph was emperor of Austria and king of Hungary; each half had its own parliament and government, but they shared foreign policy, the army, and the finances for both. Hungary's Magyar elite then pursued **Magyarization** of its own minorities. The other nationalities, especially the **Slavs** (Czechs, Poles, Croats, Serbs, Slovaks), resented being left out. Nationalism was a constant threat to the empire's survival.

## Russia: reform and reaction
Defeat in the Crimea convinced **Alexander II** (r. 1855–1881) that Russia had to modernize. In **1861** he **emancipated the serfs**, more than 20 million people. Peasants gained personal freedom and land, but they had to make **redemption payments** for decades, and the land was usually held by the village commune (*mir*), which limited mobility. He also created elected local councils (**zemstvos**, 1864), reformed the courts with jury trials, and shortened military service. Radicals were not satisfied; populists (*narodniki*) tried to stir the peasants, and the terrorist group **People's Will assassinated Alexander II in 1881**.

His son **Alexander III** responded with reaction: censorship, police repression and **Russification** (forcing Russian language and Orthodoxy on minorities). **Pogroms**, violent attacks on Jews, spread, and millions of Jews emigrated. Under **Sergei Witte**, finance minister in the 1890s, Russia industrialized rapidly with foreign loans, building the **Trans-Siberian Railway**. Defeat by Japan (1904–1905) and the **Revolution of 1905** (beginning with **Bloody Sunday**, when troops fired on peaceful petitioners) forced **Nicholas II** to issue the **October Manifesto**, promising civil rights and an elected parliament, the **Duma**, which he soon restricted (Unit 10).

## France and Britain: toward democracy
**France.** Napoleon III's **Second Empire** (1852–1870) rebuilt Paris with wide boulevards (Baron **Haussmann**), promoted railways and banks, and liberalized in the 1860s, but collapsed at Sedan. The **Third Republic** was born in defeat. In 1871 radical Parisians set up the **Paris Commune**, which the national government crushed in "Bloody Week" (about 20,000 killed), leaving lasting class bitterness. The Republic survived scandals and the nationalist general **Boulanger**. The **Dreyfus Affair** (1894–1906), in which **Captain Alfred Dreyfus**, a Jewish army officer, was falsely convicted of spying for Germany, split France between the army, Church and anti-Semitic right on one side and republicans, socialists and intellectuals on the other. **Émile Zola's** open letter "**J'accuse!**" (1898) accused the army of a cover-up. Dreyfus was cleared in 1906, and the republicans' victory led to the **separation of church and state (1905)**. The affair also convinced journalist **Theodor Herzl** that Jews needed their own state, and he founded political **Zionism** (1897).

**Britain** widened democracy by reform rather than revolution. The Conservative **Benjamin Disraeli** passed the **Second Reform Act (1867)**, enfranchising many urban working men; the Liberal **William Gladstone's** governments introduced the **secret ballot (1872)** and the **Third Reform Act (1884)** for rural workers. Gladstone repeatedly tried, and failed, to give **Home Rule** to **Ireland**. The **Labour Party** formed (1900–1906), the **Parliament Act of 1911** ended the House of Lords' veto, and the **suffragettes** led by **Emmeline Pankhurst** campaigned, sometimes militantly, for women's votes.

## Socialism and labor unions
Trade unions grew rapidly, especially "new unions" of unskilled workers after the 1889 London dock strike. Marx helped found the **First International** (1864), which split between Marxists and anarchists led by **Mikhail Bakunin**. The **Second International** (1889) united national socialist parties. The largest was Germany's **Social Democratic Party (SPD)**, which survived the Anti-Socialist Laws and became the **largest party in the Reichstag by 1912**.

Socialists argued about strategy. **Eduard Bernstein's revisionism** (*Evolutionary Socialism*, 1899) said capitalism was not collapsing and socialism could come gradually through elections and reforms. Orthodox Marxists such as Karl Kautsky insisted on revolution in theory while working within parliaments in practice. **Syndicalists** (Georges Sorel) put their faith in the general strike. In Britain, the **Fabian Society** pursued gradual, democratic socialism.

## Diplomacy, 1871–1914: Bismarck's system and its collapse
-> A unified Germany fears a French war of revenge over Alsace-Lorraine | Bismarck builds alliances to keep France isolated (Dual Alliance, Triple Alliance, Reinsurance Treaty) | William II drops Russia (1890); France and Russia ally (1894); Britain joins them: Europe splits into two armed camps

After 1871, Bismarck called Germany "satiated" and worked to keep the peace and keep France **isolated**. His tools: the **Three Emperors' League** (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Russia, 1873 and 1881); the **Congress of Berlin (1878)**, where he acted as "honest broker" after Russia defeated the Ottomans and revised the Treaty of San Stefano: Serbia, Montenegro and Romania became independent, a big Bulgaria was cut down, and Austria-Hungary was allowed to occupy **Bosnia-Herzegovina** (which angered Russia); the **Dual Alliance (1879)** with Austria-Hungary; the **Triple Alliance (1882)** adding Italy; and the secret **Reinsurance Treaty (1887)** with Russia.

William II let the Reinsurance Treaty lapse in **1890**. France quickly courted Russia with loans and signed the **Franco-Russian Alliance (1894)**, the nightmare of a two-front war Bismarck had avoided. Britain, alarmed by Germany's navy, left its "splendid isolation": the **Entente Cordiale (1904)** settled colonial quarrels with France, and the **Anglo-Russian Entente (1907)** did the same with Russia, creating the **Triple Entente**. Europe was now divided into two blocs: the **Triple Alliance** (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) and the **Triple Entente** (France, Russia, Britain).
