=== LESSON
## The big picture
In 1648 the **Peace of Westphalia** ended the Thirty Years' War, the last great war fought mainly over religion in Europe. From then on, rulers fought for power, territory and trade rather than faith. The treaty recognized the independence of the **Dutch Republic** and Switzerland, let each German prince run his own state (making the Holy Roman Emperor weak), and accepted Calvinism alongside Catholicism and Lutheranism. Historians often treat Westphalia as the birth of the modern system of **sovereign states**.

The central question of the next seventy years was simple: **who holds supreme power in a state?** Two answers competed. **Absolutism** said the monarch rules alone, answering only to God (**divine right of kings**), with no parliament able to block him. **Constitutionalism** said the ruler's power is limited by law and shared with a representative body. France under Louis XIV became the model of the first answer; England and the Dutch Republic became the models of the second.

## France: Louis XIV, the model absolute monarch
Louis XIV became king in 1643 at age four and reigned until 1715, the longest reign in European history. As a boy he lived through the **Fronde** (1648–1653), a series of revolts by nobles and the Paris law courts against the regency government of Cardinal **Mazarin**. Louis never forgot being forced to flee Paris. He drew a lesson: the nobility must be tamed.

-> Noble revolts of the Fronde humiliate the young king | Louis XIV builds Versailles and moves the court there (1682) | Great nobles trade political power for court rituals and royal favor

At **Versailles**, a giant palace outside Paris, the most powerful nobles were expected to live at court, where they competed for honors such as handing the king his shirt. Nobles busy with etiquette were not raising armies. Louis governed through middle-class officials and royal agents called **intendants**, who carried his orders to the provinces. He never called the **Estates-General**, France's national assembly, which did not meet from 1614 until 1789. The bishop **Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet** gave the theory: kings are God's lieutenants on earth. The phrase "L'état, c'est moi" ("I am the state") is attributed to Louis, though he probably never said it.

His finance minister **Jean-Baptiste Colbert** practiced **mercantilism**: the idea that a nation's wealth is a fixed pie measured in gold and silver, so the state should export more than it imports, protect home industries with tariffs, build roads and canals, and found colonies to supply raw materials.

Louis wanted "one king, one law, one faith." In 1685 he **revoked the Edict of Nantes** (which since 1598 had protected French Protestants, the **Huguenots**). About 200,000 skilled Huguenots fled to the Dutch Republic, England, Prussia and America, taking their crafts and capital with them. It was a gift to France's rivals.

Louis also fought almost constantly to push France's borders to the Rhine. His last and biggest war, the **War of the Spanish Succession** (1701–1714), began when the childless Spanish king left his throne to Louis's grandson Philip. England, the Dutch and Austria feared a French-Spanish superstate and formed a grand alliance against him. The **Treaty of Utrecht (1713)** let Philip V keep Spain but ruled that the French and Spanish crowns could never be united. Britain took **Gibraltar**, Minorca, Newfoundland, Nova Scotia (Acadia) and the **asiento** (the right to sell enslaved Africans in Spanish America). Utrecht established the principle of the **balance of power**: no single state should dominate Europe. Louis died in 1715 leaving France victorious in prestige but deeply in debt.

## England: from civil war to constitutional monarchy
England's Stuart kings, **James I** and **Charles I**, also believed in divine right, but they needed Parliament's consent to raise taxes. Charles I collected taxes without Parliament, accepted the **Petition of Right (1628)** and then ignored it, and ruled without Parliament from 1629 to 1640. A Scottish revolt over religion forced him to call Parliament again for money. That **Long Parliament** demanded reforms, and in 1642 the **English Civil War** began.

-> Charles I taxes without Parliament and pushes high-church religion on Puritans and Scots | English Civil War, 1642–1649 | Charles I executed (1649); England becomes a republic

The **Royalists** (Cavaliers) fought the **Parliamentarians** (Roundheads), who were strongest among Puritans, merchants and London. **Oliver Cromwell** led Parliament's disciplined **New Model Army** to victory. In 1649 Charles I was tried and **beheaded**, a shocking act in a Europe of kings. England became a republic, the **Commonwealth**. Radical groups appeared, such as the **Levellers**, who wanted votes for most men. Cromwell dissolved Parliament and ruled as **Lord Protector** (1653–1658), a military dictatorship in practice. Puritan rule (theaters closed, strict morality) grew unpopular.

After Cromwell died, the monarchy was **restored in 1660** under **Charles II**. Parliament now held the upper hand on taxes. Two political factions formed: **Whigs**, who wanted to limit the king and exclude Catholics from the throne, and **Tories**, who supported royal authority and hereditary right. The **Test Act (1673)** barred non-Anglicans from public office.

-> Catholic James II rules by decree and his son is born, promising a Catholic dynasty | Glorious Revolution (1688): Parliament invites William of Orange and Mary | Bill of Rights (1689) makes Parliament supreme: constitutional monarchy

Charles's brother **James II** was openly Catholic and ignored laws he disliked. When his son was born in 1688, Parliament's leaders invited the Protestant Dutch prince **William of Orange** and his wife **Mary** (James's Protestant daughter) to take the throne. James fled with almost no bloodshed: the **Glorious Revolution**. William and Mary accepted the **Bill of Rights (1689)**: no taxes or standing army in peacetime without Parliament's consent, free elections, free speech in Parliament, and no Catholic monarch. The **Toleration Act (1689)** allowed Protestant dissenters to worship (but not Catholics). **John Locke's** *Two Treatises of Government* (1690) justified the revolution: government rests on the consent of the governed. The **Act of Union (1707)** joined England and Scotland as **Great Britain**.

## The Dutch Republic: a merchant republic
The **United Provinces of the Netherlands** won independence from Spain (recognized in 1648). It was a loose federation of seven provinces with a weak central **States General**. Power lay with wealthy merchant oligarchs called **regents**, while the princes of the House of **Orange** held the office of **stadtholder** (military leader) and gained power in wartime crises. In the "disaster year" of **1672**, when France invaded, the Dutch turned to **William III of Orange**, the same William who later took England's throne.

The seventeenth century was the **Dutch Golden Age**. The **Dutch East India Company** (VOC, founded 1602) dominated the Asian spice trade; Amsterdam had the world's leading stock exchange and the **Bank of Amsterdam**; Dutch ships carried much of Europe's trade. The Republic practiced unusual **religious toleration**, sheltering Jews, Huguenots and dissenters like the philosopher **Spinoza**. Painters such as **Rembrandt** and **Vermeer** painted merchants and everyday life rather than kings and saints. By 1700, wars with England and France had drained the Dutch, and Britain overtook them as the leading commercial power.

## Central and Eastern Europe: Austria, Prussia, Russia
After 1648 the Habsburg emperors in Vienna gave up on controlling Germany and built a Danube empire: Austria, Bohemia and Hungary. In **1683 the Ottoman Turks besieged Vienna** for the last time; a relief army led by the Polish king **Jan Sobieski** broke the siege. In the **Treaty of Karlowitz (1699)** the Ottomans surrendered most of **Hungary** to Austria. The Habsburg lands stayed a patchwork of peoples and laws held together by the dynasty, the Catholic Church and the army, never as centralized as France.

**Brandenburg-Prussia**, ruled by the **Hohenzollern** family, was poor and scattered across northern Germany. **Frederick William, the Great Elector** (r. 1640–1688), built a large standing army and a tax bureaucracy (the General War Commissariat). He made a bargain with the landowning nobles, the **Junkers**: they accepted royal taxes and served as army officers in return for total control over their serfs. His son became **King in Prussia** in 1701. The Great Elector had also welcomed Huguenot refugees from France with the **Edict of Potsdam (1685)**, gaining skilled craftsmen for Berlin. Prussia became famous as an army with a state attached.

In Russia, **Peter the Great** (r. 1682–1725) toured Western Europe incognito in the **Grand Embassy** (1697–1698) to study shipbuilding and technology. Back home he forced **westernization** from the top: nobles had to shave their beards (or pay a beard tax) and wear Western clothes; he built a navy; he crushed the rebellious palace guard (the **streltsy**); he created the **Table of Ranks** (1722), which ranked nobles by state service rather than birth; and he put the Orthodox Church under a government committee, the **Holy Synod**. He fought Sweden in the **Great Northern War** (1700–1721), won the decisive **Battle of Poltava (1709)** against Charles XII, and gained the Baltic coast in the **Treaty of Nystad (1721)**. There he built his new capital, **St. Petersburg** (founded 1703), his "window on the West." Westernization touched the elite; for peasants, **serfdom** grew harsher.

## How it fits together
Every state faced the same pressure: war had become enormously expensive (standing armies, navies, fortresses). Rulers who could raise money without asking anyone, as in France, Prussia and Russia, became absolutist. Where a parliament controlled taxes, as in England, or merchants controlled the state, as in the Dutch Republic, power was shared under law. Exam questions often ask you to match a state to its model and to name the document or event that defined it.
