=== LESSON
## The big picture
For all of human history before about 1750, almost everyone farmed, energy came from muscles, wind, water and wood, and economies grew very slowly. The **Industrial Revolution**, which began in **Britain** in the late 1700s, changed that forever. Machines powered by **coal and steam** produced goods in **factories**; output per worker multiplied; people moved to cities; and new social classes and political demands appeared. This unit covers why it began, how it worked, who gained and lost, and how Britain responded with reform laws and the Chartist movement.

## Step one: the agricultural revolution
-> Enclosure, new crops and crop rotation make farms more productive | More food with fewer farm workers | Population grows and surplus rural labor moves to factory towns

British landowners **enclosed** the old open fields and common lands into compact private farms, especially through acts of Parliament in the 1700s and early 1800s. Enclosure let improving landlords experiment, but it pushed many small farmers and cottagers off the land. New methods spread: **crop rotation** with **turnips and clover** (popularized by Charles "Turnip" **Townshend**) restored nitrogen to the soil and fed livestock through the winter, so land no longer had to lie fallow. **Jethro Tull's seed drill** (1701) planted seeds in neat rows, and Robert **Bakewell** bred heavier sheep and cattle. Higher yields fed a growing population and freed workers for industry.

## Why Britain first?
Britain had an unusual combination of advantages:
- **Natural resources:** abundant **coal** and **iron ore**, often close together, and many rivers for water power and transport.
- **Capital and finance:** profits from overseas trade (including the slave trade and plantations), a sophisticated banking system and the Bank of England.
- **Markets:** a growing home population and a large colonial empire; no internal tolls or tariffs.
- **Stable government friendly to business:** after 1688, Parliament protected property, enforced patents, built canals and roads, and supported a navy that protected trade.
- **Labor:** population growth and enclosure created a supply of workers.
- **Culture:** a practical tinkering tradition and entrepreneurs, many of them Protestant **Dissenters** shut out of politics, who poured energy into business.

## Cotton, steam and iron
The first factory industry was **cotton textiles**. Each invention created a bottleneck that the next solved:

-> John Kay's flying shuttle (1733) speeds up weaving, so weavers need more thread | Hargreaves's spinning jenny (c. 1765), Arkwright's water frame (1769) and Crompton's mule (1779) mechanize spinning | Machines become too big for cottages: the factory system is born

**Richard Arkwright's water frame** needed a river to turn it, so he built one of the first true **factories** at Cromford (1771), gathering workers under one roof and one clock. Edmund Cartwright's **power loom** (1785) then mechanized weaving. The raw cotton came largely from slave plantations in the **American South**, where Eli Whitney's **cotton gin** (1793) made production cheap.

**Steam** freed factories from rivers. Thomas **Newcomen's** steam engine (1712) pumped water out of coal mines but wasted fuel. **James Watt**, with his partner Matthew Boulton, added a **separate condenser** (patented 1769) and later rotary motion, producing an efficient engine that could power any machine anywhere. Factories clustered in coal towns such as **Manchester**, nicknamed "Cottonopolis."

**Iron** improved too. **Abraham Darby** smelted iron using **coke** (baked coal) instead of scarce charcoal (1709), and Henry Cort's **puddling** process (1784) made better wrought iron cheaply.

**Railways** combined coal, iron and steam. The Stockton and Darlington line opened in 1825; **George Stephenson's Rocket** won the trials for the **Liverpool and Manchester Railway** (1830), the first intercity line powered by steam. A railway boom in the 1840s cut transport costs, created huge demand for iron and coal, and knit national markets together.

## The spread to the continent
**Belgium**, with coal and iron and close ties to Britain, industrialized first on the continent, then northern France and the German states. Continental countries had obstacles: wars until 1815, poorer transport, internal tolls and guild rules. Governments played a bigger role than in Britain, building railways and setting tariffs. The Prussian-led **Zollverein** (customs union, 1834) removed tariffs among most German states. The German economist **Friedrich List** argued that young industries need **protective tariffs** to compete with Britain. Southern and eastern Europe, including Russia, industrialized only late in the century.

## Social impact: a new world of work and cities
-> Factories and steam power concentrate work in industrial towns | Rapid urbanization: overcrowded slums, polluted water, cholera, long hours, child labor | A new working class and a new middle class, each with political demands

**Factory work** imposed strict discipline: fixed hours set by the clock and bell, fines for lateness, 12- to 14-hour days, six days a week. Employers hired **women and children** because they were cheaper and nimble at tending machines; children as young as six worked in mills and mines. Families that had worked together at home in the putting-out system were split up during the working day.

**Cities** grew faster than anyone could plan for. Manchester went from about 25,000 people in 1770 to over 300,000 by 1850. Workers crowded into **back-to-back houses** with no running water or sewers; **cholera** epidemics struck in 1832 and 1848. **Friedrich Engels**, a German whose family owned a Manchester mill, described the horrors in ***The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845)**, which shaped Marx's thinking.

Industrialization created a **working class (proletariat)** of wage earners who owned nothing but their labor, and an expanding **middle class** of factory owners, merchants and professionals. The middle class prized hard work, thrift, self-help and respectability, and promoted the ideal of "**separate spheres**": men in the public world of work, women in the home. Historians still debate the **standard of living**: real wages fell or stagnated for many workers until about the 1840s, then rose; the long-term gains were enormous, but the first generations paid a heavy price.

Economists explained (and often justified) the new order. **Thomas Malthus** (*Essay on the Principle of Population*, 1798) argued that population grows faster than the food supply, so poverty is inevitable unless people have fewer children. **David Ricardo** proposed the "**iron law of wages**": wages tend to sink to subsistence level. Their gloom earned economics the label "the dismal science." These **classical economists** opposed government intervention in the economy.

## Workers' responses and British reform
Workers resisted. The **Luddites** (1811–1816) smashed textile machines that threatened their jobs. Trade unions were banned by the **Combination Acts** (1799–1800), repealed in 1824. In 1819 cavalry charged a peaceful crowd demanding parliamentary reform in Manchester, killing about fifteen people: the **Peterloo Massacre**.

Yet Britain avoided revolution by passing reforms step by step:
- **Catholic Emancipation (1829)** let Catholics sit in Parliament.
- The **Great Reform Act of 1832** took seats from "**rotten boroughs**" (tiny or empty districts controlled by patrons) and gave them to new industrial cities, and gave the vote to middle-class men who owned or rented property of moderate value. The electorate grew by roughly half, to about one in five adult men in England. Workers were still excluded.
- The **Factory Act of 1833** banned children under 9 from textile mills, limited hours for children, and created government **inspectors** to enforce it. The **Mines Act (1842)** barred women and children under 10 from underground work; the **Ten Hours Act (1847)** limited women and teenagers to ten hours a day.
- The **Poor Law Amendment Act (1834)** forced the able-bodied poor into harsh **workhouses** to discourage dependence, a reform workers hated.
- Slavery was abolished throughout the British Empire in **1833**.
- The **Corn Laws** (1815) had protected landowners with high tariffs on imported grain, keeping bread prices high. The middle-class **Anti-Corn Law League** (Richard Cobden, John Bright) campaigned for free trade, and Prime Minister Robert **Peel** repealed the Corn Laws in **1846**, a victory for industrialists and free trade over the landed aristocracy.

## Chartism
Disappointed that the Reform Act of 1832 left them voteless, working-class radicals drew up the **People's Charter (1838)**. Its six points: **universal male suffrage**, the **secret ballot**, no property qualification for members of Parliament, **payment of MPs**, equal electoral districts, and annual elections to Parliament. Chartists gathered millions of signatures on petitions in 1839, 1842 and 1848; Parliament rejected all three, and the movement faded after 1848. But Chartism was the first mass working-class political movement, and by 1918 every point except annual parliaments had become law.
