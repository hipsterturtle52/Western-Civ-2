=== LESSON
## The big picture
Between 1850 and 1914 Europe reached the peak of its power and confidence. A **Second Industrial Revolution** built on steel, chemicals, electricity and oil made Europe and the United States the workshops of the world. Population soared, cities were cleaned up, and tens of millions emigrated. Ordinary people became literate voters reading cheap newspapers: the age of **mass politics**. Europeans used their new technology to seize most of Africa and much of Asia in the **New Imperialism**. Yet at the same time, scientists and artists such as **Darwin, Freud, Nietzsche, Einstein and Picasso** undermined older certainties about humanity, reason and reality. This unit mixes economics, empire and culture; expect "match the thinker to the idea" and "what motivated imperialism" questions.

## The Second Industrial Revolution
-> New processes and science-based industry: Bessemer steel (1856), synthetic chemicals, electricity, the internal combustion engine | Mass production, big corporations and cartels; Germany and the U.S. overtake Britain | A global economy, consumer goods, and fierce competition for markets and raw materials

The first industrial revolution ran on cotton, coal, iron and steam. The second (about **1870–1914**) ran on:
- **Steel:** Henry Bessemer's converter (1856) made cheap steel for railways, ships, bridges and weapons.
- **Chemicals:** German firms led in synthetic dyes, fertilizers, pharmaceuticals and explosives, often employing university-trained scientists.
- **Electricity:** dynamos and power stations; Thomas Edison's practical light bulb (1879); electric trams and factory motors.
- **Oil and the internal combustion engine:** Gottlieb Daimler and Karl Benz built early automobiles in the 1880s; Rudolf Diesel his engine in the 1890s.
- **Communications:** the telephone (Bell, 1876) and radio (Marconi, 1890s), plus undersea telegraph cables.

**Germany** became the industrial leader of the continent and, with the **United States**, overtook Britain in steel production by about 1900. Businesses grew into giant corporations; in Germany, firms formed **cartels** that fixed prices and shared markets. A long slump in prices and profits, the **Long Depression** (about 1873–1896), led most countries except Britain to abandon free trade for **protective tariffs** (Germany in 1879). Wages slowly rose; department stores, advertising and brand-name goods created a **consumer society** for the middle class and better-off workers.

## Demography: more people, healthier cities, mass migration
Europe's population rose from about **266 million in 1850 to more than 450 million by 1914**. The main reason was again a falling death rate, now helped by real medical and sanitary advances. **Louis Pasteur** proved the **germ theory of disease** in the 1860s (and developed pasteurization and vaccines); **Robert Koch** identified the bacteria causing tuberculosis (1882) and cholera; **Joseph Lister** introduced antiseptic surgery (1867). Governments built **sewers and clean water systems**; after London's "Great Stink" of 1858, engineers built a modern sewer network. In Paris, Baron **Haussmann** replaced slums with broad boulevards, parks and sewers. Late in the century, birth rates also began to fall, first in France, as middle-class families chose to have fewer children.

Even so, Europe could not employ everyone. Between 1815 and 1914 about **50 million Europeans emigrated**, mostly to the **United States**, Canada, Argentina, Brazil and Australia. Early waves came from Ireland (driven by the **potato famine** of 1845–1852, which killed about a million people) and Germany; later waves from **Italy**, Austria-Hungary and the Russian Empire, including many Jews fleeing pogroms.

## Mass politics and society
By 1914 most Western European men could vote. To vote sensibly, the argument went, citizens needed schools: states created **free, compulsory primary education** (Britain from 1870; France under the **Ferry laws** of the 1880s, which made schools secular). Literacy brought a cheap **mass press** (Britain's *Daily Mail*, 1896) that often whipped up nationalism. **Mass political parties** with dues-paying members, newspapers and clubs replaced loose groups of notables: socialist parties on the left, Catholic parties, and new **radical-right** movements that mixed nationalism, anti-Semitism and hostility to liberalism (for example, Karl Lueger, the anti-Semitic mayor of Vienna).

Women organized too. **Feminists** campaigned for property rights, education, entry into professions and the vote. The **suffragists** used petitions and persuasion; the British **suffragettes** of Emmeline Pankhurst's Women's Social and Political Union (founded 1903) used militant tactics. Before 1914 only **Finland (1906)** and **Norway (1913)** gave women the national vote in Europe.

## The New Imperialism, 1880–1914
-> Industrial economies want raw materials, markets and investment outlets; nationalist rivalry; social Darwinist and "civilizing" ideas | The Scramble for Africa and expansion in Asia, made possible by steamships, quinine, railways and the machine gun | By 1914 Europeans control about 90 percent of Africa; colonized peoples suffer exploitation and violence; rivalries worsen tensions in Europe

Europeans had held coastal trading posts for centuries. After about 1880, they rushed to conquer and directly rule vast interiors. Historians list several **motives**:
- **Economic:** raw materials (rubber, cotton, copper, palm oil), new markets and investment opportunities. The British economist **J. A. Hobson** (*Imperialism*, 1902) argued that capitalists pushed imperialism to find profitable investments; **Lenin** later called imperialism "the highest stage of capitalism" (1916).
- **Political and strategic:** national prestige, coaling stations and naval bases, and fear of rivals grabbing land first.
- **Ideological:** **social Darwinism**, the misapplication of Darwin's ideas to claim that some races and nations were "fitter" than others; the "**civilizing mission**"; and Christian **missionaries**. **Rudyard Kipling's** poem "**The White Man's Burden**" (1899) urged Americans and Britons to rule "new-caught, sullen peoples" as a duty.
- **Enablers:** the steamship, telegraph and railway; **quinine**, which protected Europeans against malaria; and the **Maxim gun**, an early machine gun. As one poet put it, "Whatever happens, we have got the Maxim gun, and they have not."

**Africa.** To avoid war over Africa, Bismarck hosted the **Berlin Conference (1884–1885)**, which set the rule that a power must "effectively occupy" territory to claim it. No Africans were invited. Within twenty years almost the whole continent was divided; only **Ethiopia**, which crushed an Italian army at **Adwa (1896)**, and **Liberia** stayed independent. King **Leopold II of Belgium** ran the **Congo Free State** as his personal property, forcing Africans to gather rubber under a regime of terror and mutilation that killed millions; international outrage forced Belgium to take over the colony in 1908. Britain bought shares in the **Suez Canal** (1875), occupied **Egypt** (1882), and dreamed (with **Cecil Rhodes**) of a "Cape to Cairo" railway. British and French expansion collided at **Fashoda** in Sudan (1898); France backed down. Britain fought the Dutch-descended **Boers** in the **South African (Boer) War (1899–1902)**, using concentration camps in which tens of thousands of Boer and African civilians died. In German Southwest Africa (Namibia), the army's campaign against the **Herero and Nama** (1904–1907) killed most of the Herero people, now widely recognized as genocide.

**Asia.** In **India**, the **Indian Rebellion (Sepoy Mutiny) of 1857** led Britain to end East India Company rule; from **1858** the British crown ruled directly (the **Raj**), and Queen Victoria became Empress of India in 1876. In **China**, Britain's victory in the **Opium War (1839–1842)** forced open ports and gave it **Hong Kong**; later "unequal treaties" carved China into foreign **spheres of influence**, and the anti-foreign **Boxer Rebellion (1900)** was crushed by an international force. France took **Indochina** (Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos). **Japan** responded differently: after the **Meiji Restoration (1868)** it rapidly modernized on Western lines, defeated China (1895) and then **Russia (1905)**, the first modern Asian victory over a European great power.

## Science and thought: new certainties and new doubts
**Charles Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859)** argued that species evolve through **natural selection**: individuals with traits better suited to their environment survive and reproduce. *The Descent of Man* (1871) placed humans in the same process. Darwin shocked religious believers who read Genesis literally. **Herbert Spencer** coined "**survival of the fittest**" and applied it to society, giving rise to **social Darwinism**, used to justify laissez-faire, imperialism and racism.

Physics overturned the Newtonian world: **Wilhelm Röntgen** discovered X-rays (1895); **Marie Curie** (with Pierre Curie) studied **radioactivity** and won two Nobel Prizes; **Max Planck** proposed quantum theory (1900); **Albert Einstein's** theory of **relativity** (1905) showed that time and space are not absolute.

**Sigmund Freud**, a Viennese doctor, founded **psychoanalysis** (*The Interpretation of Dreams*, 1900). He argued that human behavior is driven largely by the **unconscious**, by repressed desires (especially sexual and aggressive drives), and he described the mind as the id, ego and superego. Humans were less rational than the Enlightenment had believed.

**Friedrich Nietzsche** attacked Christianity and middle-class morality as "slave morality," declared "**God is dead**," praised the **will to power**, and imagined a "superman" (*Übermensch*) who would create his own values. Others built new social sciences: Auguste Comte's **positivism** (only scientific knowledge is real), and the sociologists **Émile Durkheim** and **Max Weber** (*The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*, 1905).

## Literature and the arts: from Realism to Modernism
Mid-century **Realism** rejected Romantic idealism to show ordinary life truthfully: **Gustave Courbet** painted laborers (*The Stone Breakers*); novelists **Gustave Flaubert** (*Madame Bovary*, 1857), **Charles Dickens**, **Leo Tolstoy** and **Émile Zola** (whose "naturalism" showed people shaped by heredity and environment) described society in detail. Henrik **Ibsen's** play *A Doll's House* (1879) challenged women's roles in marriage.

**Impressionists** such as **Claude Monet** and Pierre-Auguste Renoir (first group show 1874) painted outdoors to capture fleeting effects of **light and color**. **Post-Impressionists** (Paul **Cézanne**, Vincent **van Gogh**, Paul Gauguin) used color and form to express structure and emotion. By 1914 **Modernism** broke with tradition entirely: **Pablo Picasso** and Georges Braque invented **Cubism**, showing objects from several viewpoints at once (*Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*, 1907); Expressionists painted inner anxiety; and **Igor Stravinsky's** ballet ***The Rite of Spring*** caused a riot at its 1913 Paris premiere. Artists and thinkers alike suggested that the world was less orderly and rational than Europe's confident leaders believed.
