=== LESSON
## The big picture
The **Second World War (1939–1945)** killed about **60 million people**, more than half of them civilians, and included the **Holocaust**, the Nazi murder of six million Jews. Europe emerged in ruins and was split for 45 years by the **Cold War** between the American-led West and the Soviet-dominated East. Western Europe rebuilt with American aid, created welfare states, gave up its colonial empires, and began to unite economically in what became the **European Union**. In **1989–1991** communism collapsed in Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union itself. This unit covers a lot of ground quickly; focus on turning points and on the big postwar trends.

## The war in Europe, 1939–1941: Axis triumphs
-> Hitler invades Poland after securing the Nazi-Soviet Pact | Blitzkrieg conquers Poland (1939), then Denmark, Norway, the Low Countries and France (1940) | Britain stands alone; Hitler turns east and attacks the USSR (June 1941)

Germany invaded **Poland on September 1, 1939**, using **Blitzkrieg** ("lightning war"): fast tanks and motorized infantry coordinated with dive-bombers. The Soviets invaded eastern Poland on September 17, as the secret protocol of the Nazi-Soviet Pact allowed. After a quiet winter (the "Phoney War"), Germany took **Denmark and Norway** (April 1940), then attacked the Netherlands, Belgium and **France (May 1940)**, sending tanks through the Ardennes forest and around France's fortified **Maginot Line**. More than 300,000 British and French troops were evacuated from **Dunkirk**. **France surrendered in June 1940**. Germany occupied the north and west, and the collaborationist **Vichy regime** of Marshal **Philippe Pétain** governed the south. General **Charles de Gaulle** led the **Free French** from London. Italy entered the war on Germany's side in June 1940.

Britain, under Prime Minister **Winston Churchill** (from May 1940), fought on alone. In the **Battle of Britain** (summer and fall 1940), the Royal Air Force, aided by **radar**, defeated the German air force's attempt to win control of the skies before an invasion. Germany then bombed British cities in **the Blitz**. The neutral United States supplied Britain through **Lend-Lease** (1941).

On **June 22, 1941**, Hitler launched **Operation Barbarossa**, the invasion of the **Soviet Union**, seeking *Lebensraum*, oil and the destruction of "Judeo-Bolshevism." German armies reached the outskirts of **Moscow** and besieged **Leningrad** (a siege of nearly 900 days in which about a million civilians died), but were halted by Soviet resistance and winter in December 1941. That same month Japan attacked **Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941)**, and Hitler declared war on the **United States**. The war was now global, and the "Grand Alliance" of Britain, the USSR and the U.S. outweighed the Axis in men and industry.

## Turning points and Allied victory, 1942–1945
-> Germany overextends in Russia and North Africa; the U.S. joins the war | Stalingrad (1942–43), El Alamein (1942), Kursk (1943); Allied invasions of Italy (1943) and Normandy (1944) | Germany is crushed between the Soviet and Western armies; surrender May 8, 1945

The decisive turning point was **Stalingrad** (August 1942–February 1943): the entire German **Sixth Army** was encircled and surrendered. At **El Alamein** (late 1942) the British under Montgomery defeated Rommel's forces in Egypt, and Anglo-American forces landed in North Africa. At **Kursk** (July 1943), the largest tank battle in history, the Soviets ended Germany's ability to attack in the east. The Allies invaded **Sicily and Italy (1943)**; Mussolini was overthrown, and Italy surrendered (though German troops fought on there). On **D-Day, June 6, 1944**, Allied forces under **Dwight Eisenhower** landed in **Normandy**, opening the long-promised **second front** in the west. Paris was liberated in August 1944. Germany's last offensive, the **Battle of the Bulge** (December 1944), failed. Soviet troops stormed **Berlin**; **Hitler killed himself on April 30, 1945**, and Germany surrendered on **May 8, 1945 (V-E Day)**. Japan surrendered in August 1945 after the United States dropped **atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki** and the USSR declared war on it.

## The Holocaust
The Nazis' racial war reached its climax in the **Holocaust**. After the invasion of the USSR, mobile killing squads (**Einsatzgruppen**) shot over a million Jews in occupied Soviet lands (at Babi Yar near Kiev, 33,000 in two days). Jews in Poland were crowded into sealed **ghettos**, where many starved; in 1943 the **Warsaw Ghetto uprising** fought back for weeks. At the **Wannsee Conference (January 1942)** senior officials coordinated the "**Final Solution**," the plan to murder all of Europe's Jews. Jews from across Europe were deported by train to **death camps** in occupied Poland, such as **Auschwitz-Birkenau**, Treblinka and Sobibor, where most were murdered in gas chambers on arrival. About **six million Jews**, two-thirds of Europe's Jewish population, were killed, along with **Roma** (Gypsies), disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war, Polish elites, gay men and political opponents. After the war the **Nuremberg Trials (1945–1946)** tried Nazi leaders for **crimes against humanity**, establishing that "following orders" is no defense.

## Wartime conferences and the division of Europe
The "Big Three," **Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin**, met at **Tehran (1943)** and **Yalta (February 1945)**. At Yalta they agreed to divide Germany into **occupation zones**, to create the **United Nations**, and that the USSR would enter the war against Japan; Stalin promised **free elections** in Eastern Europe. At **Potsdam (July–August 1945)**, with Truman and (after an election) Clement Attlee replacing Roosevelt and Churchill, they agreed on Germany's **demilitarization and denazification**, moved Poland's borders westward, and approved the expulsion of millions of Germans from eastern Europe.

Stalin did not keep the Yalta promise. With the Red Army in control, communist parties took power across **Eastern Europe** (Poland, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia by 1948, East Germany), creating Soviet **satellite states**. Yugoslavia's communist leader **Tito**, who had liberated his country largely by himself, broke with Stalin in 1948 and stayed independent. In March 1946 Churchill warned that "an **iron curtain** has descended across the continent."

## The Cold War in Europe
-> Soviet control of Eastern Europe and fear of communist expansion | U.S. containment: Truman Doctrine (1947), Marshall Plan (1947), NATO (1949) | Europe divided into two blocs; Germany split (1949); Soviets answer with Comecon and the Warsaw Pact (1955)

The United States adopted **containment**: stopping communism from spreading. The **Truman Doctrine (1947)** gave aid to **Greece and Turkey** against communist pressure. The **Marshall Plan** (European Recovery Program, 1948–1952) gave about **$13 billion** to rebuild Western Europe's economies; Stalin forbade Eastern Europe to accept it and created **Comecon** (1949) instead. When the Western powers merged their zones of Germany and introduced a new currency, Stalin **blockaded West Berlin (1948–1949)**; the Allies supplied the city for nearly a year by the **Berlin Airlift**. In 1949 Germany split into the **Federal Republic of Germany** (West) and the **German Democratic Republic** (East). The West formed **NATO (1949)**; the Soviet bloc answered with the **Warsaw Pact (1955)**. The USSR tested an atomic bomb in 1949, beginning the nuclear arms race.

After Stalin's death (1953), **Nikita Khrushchev** denounced Stalin's crimes in his "**Secret Speech**" (1956), beginning **de-Stalinization**. But the limits of change were clear: when **Hungary** tried to leave the Warsaw Pact in **1956** under Imre Nagy, Soviet tanks crushed the revolution. East Germans fled west in such numbers that in **August 1961** the East German regime built the **Berlin Wall**. The superpowers came closest to nuclear war in the **Cuban Missile Crisis (1962)**. In **Czechoslovakia** in **1968**, the reform communist **Alexander Dubček** launched the **Prague Spring**, promising "socialism with a human face"; Warsaw Pact armies invaded, and the **Brezhnev Doctrine** declared the Soviet right to intervene to protect socialism in any bloc country. The 1970s brought **détente**: West German Chancellor **Willy Brandt's Ostpolitik** opened relations with the East, and the **Helsinki Accords (1975)** recognized Europe's postwar borders while committing all signers, including the USSR, to respect **human rights**, a promise dissidents later used.

## Western Europe: recovery, welfare states and change
Western Europe enjoyed an unprecedented boom from about 1950 to 1973. West Germany's "**economic miracle**" under Chancellor **Konrad Adenauer** and economics minister Ludwig Erhard rebuilt a democratic, prosperous state. **Christian Democratic** parties (Adenauer in Germany, De Gasperi in Italy) and social democrats dominated politics. Following the wartime **Beveridge Report**, Britain's Labour government under **Clement Attlee** (1945–1951) built a **welfare state**: national insurance, nationalized industries and the **National Health Service (1948)**. Across the West, governments used **Keynesian** policies and provided health care, pensions and unemployment benefits.

In France, the weak Fourth Republic collapsed over the Algerian war, and **de Gaulle** founded the **Fifth Republic (1958)** with a strong presidency; he pursued an independent foreign policy and took France out of NATO's military command (1966). In **May 1968**, student protests and a general strike shook France, part of a wave of youth rebellion across the West. Women gained the vote in France (1944) and Italy (1946); **Simone de Beauvoir's** *The Second Sex* (1949) inspired the later feminist movement. **Existentialist** writers such as **Jean-Paul Sartre** and **Albert Camus** argued that in a world without given meaning, individuals must create their own through choices. Labor shortages brought **guest workers** and immigrants from Turkey, North Africa, the Caribbean and South Asia, making Western Europe more diverse. The oil shocks of 1973 and 1979 ended the boom, and in the 1980s leaders such as **Margaret Thatcher** in Britain privatized industries and cut back the state.

## Decolonization
-> World War II exhausts the colonial powers and discredits racial empire; nationalist movements grow; both superpowers oppose old empires | Independence for India (1947), Indonesia (1949), most of Africa (1950s–60s); wars in Indochina and Algeria | Europe loses its global empires; Britain and France reorient toward Europe

Britain granted independence to **India and Pakistan in 1947**, after a long nonviolent campaign led by **Mohandas Gandhi**; partition along religious lines caused mass violence and migration. The Dutch left **Indonesia** (1949). France fought to keep **Indochina** until its defeat at **Dien Bien Phu (1954)**, and then fought a brutal war in **Algeria (1954–1962)**, home to a million European settlers, until de Gaulle granted independence. In the **Suez Crisis (1956)**, Britain, France and Israel invaded Egypt after **Gamal Abdel Nasser** nationalized the Suez Canal; pressure from the **United States and the USSR** forced them to withdraw, showing that the old colonial powers could no longer act alone. **Ghana** (1957) was the first sub-Saharan colony to gain independence; seventeen African states became independent in **1960** alone, including the Belgian Congo. By the 1970s the European empires were essentially gone.

## European integration
-> Two world wars convince leaders that French-German rivalry must end | Schuman Plan → European Coal and Steel Community (1951) → Treaty of Rome creates the European Economic Community (1957) | A common market grows into the European Union (Maastricht, 1992) with a common currency, the euro

French planners **Jean Monnet** and **Robert Schuman** proposed pooling French and German coal and steel, the raw materials of war. The **European Coal and Steel Community (1951)** joined France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg ("the Six"). The **Treaty of Rome (1957)** created the **European Economic Community (EEC)**, or **Common Market**, removing tariffs among members and creating a common external tariff. **Britain** at first stayed out; de Gaulle **vetoed** its applications in 1963 and 1967, fearing Britain would be an American "Trojan horse" and challenge French leadership, and it joined only in **1973** (with Ireland and Denmark). The **Maastricht Treaty (1992)** created the **European Union**, with common citizenship and plans for a single currency; the **euro** was introduced in 1999 (notes and coins in 2002). After 2004 the EU expanded into former communist Eastern Europe. In 2016 British voters chose to leave, and the United Kingdom left the EU in 2020 ("Brexit").

## The collapse of communism, 1980–1991
In **Poland**, the election of a Polish pope, **John Paul II (1978)**, inspired national pride, and in **1980** shipyard workers in Gdańsk led by **Lech Wałęsa** founded **Solidarity**, the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc. The regime imposed martial law (1981), but Solidarity survived underground.

-> A stagnant Soviet economy and a costly arms race and war in Afghanistan | Gorbachev's glasnost and perestroika; he abandons the Brezhnev Doctrine | Peaceful revolutions in Eastern Europe (1989), German reunification (1990), the end of the USSR (1991)

**Mikhail Gorbachev** became Soviet leader in **1985**. He introduced **glasnost** ("openness," freer speech and press) and **perestroika** ("restructuring" the economy), and signed arms-reduction treaties with the U.S. Crucially, he made clear that the USSR would **not use force** to keep Eastern European regimes in power, abandoning the Brezhnev Doctrine. In **1989** the dominoes fell: Poland held partly free elections, which Solidarity swept; Hungary opened its border with Austria; on **November 9, 1989**, the **Berlin Wall** was opened; Czechoslovakia's peaceful **Velvet Revolution** made the dissident playwright **Václav Havel** president; and in Romania, the dictator Nicolae Ceaușescu was overthrown and executed. **Germany reunified on October 3, 1990**. In **August 1991** communist hardliners tried a **coup** against Gorbachev; it failed when crowds led by Boris **Yeltsin** resisted. The Soviet republics declared independence, and the **USSR dissolved in December 1991**.

The end of the Cold War was not peaceful everywhere. **Yugoslavia** broke apart in wars (1991–1999) marked by "ethnic cleansing," including the massacre of about 8,000 Bosnian Muslim men and boys at **Srebrenica (1995)**; NATO intervened in Bosnia and later Kosovo. In the twenty-first century, Europe faced the 2008 financial crisis and the eurozone debt crisis, large-scale migration, and a renewed confrontation with Russia under **Vladimir Putin**, which annexed Crimea (2014) and launched a full-scale invasion of **Ukraine (2022)**.
